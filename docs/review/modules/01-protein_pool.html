protein_pool ArgusUNPUBLISHED_v01

The building block every protein in the game's cell is made of: a pool of copies of one protein that the cell builds, keeps, degrades and (when asked) actively removes, with what each of those costs in ATP. Every slider in the game's cell panel ends up driving one of these.

Generated report: version page · module page (the module page also lists the sibling version membrane_share_ArgusUNPUBLISHED_v01, reviewed next)

What it models

  • State: one number, the expression m: copies relative to the calibrated cell (n = n₀·m; n₀ = 682 copies in the default instance, which is NhaA).
  • Target: the game sets m_target; regulation (if switched on) lowers it to the goal m_goal = m_target·R_reg.
  • Rising: towards the goal with the mRNA's mean lifetime, τ = t½,mRNA / ln 2 ≈ 7 min, at most as fast as the cell's ribosomes allow (share s_build, set by protein_expression).
  • Natural turnover: copies degrade at k_deg ≈ 1%/h and are replaced up to the goal (only as far as protein synthesis is allowed: R_syn).
  • Lowering: copies above the goal are removed by regulated, ATP-dependent proteolysis with τ_prot ≈ 7 min, as far as the cell's protease capacity allows (s_prot).
  • Energy: raising costs ATP (amino acids from glucose plus peptide bonds), holding costs upkeep every second, removal costs ATP and returns the amino acids to a recycled pool that makes later building cheaper.
  • Everything slows as ATP runs out through min(f_ATP, 1), where f_ATP = ATP/(K_m+ATP) · (K_m+ATP_ref)/ATP_ref (1 at the reference ATP).

The equations

dm/dt   = r_build + r_down − r_prot
r_build = r_up · s_build · min(f_ATP,1)            r_up = max(m_goal − m, 0)/τ   (rising)
r_deg   = k_deg · m · min(f_ATP,1)                 (natural degradation)
r_rep   = k_deg · R_syn · min(m, m_goal) · min(f_ATP,1)   (replacement, up to the goal)
r_down  = r_rep − r_deg  ≤ 0
r_prot  = max(m − m_goal, 0)/τ_prot · s_prot · min(f_ATP,1)   (regulated removal)

J_keep  = n₀ L ((c_poly + c_recycle) r_rep + c_deg r_deg) / N_A      upkeep  [mol ATP/s]
J_build = r_build n₀ L (c_syn + c_poly)/N_A − J_saved                building
J_saved = (c_syn − c_recycle) · φ_aa · n₀ L r_build / N_A            recycled amino acids
J_prot  = c_deg n₀ L r_prot / N_A                                    removal
Q_aa_rel = n₀ L (r_prot − r_down)        amino acids returned to the cell's pool  [aa/s]

L = protein length (388 aa for NhaA). At the goal with nothing regulated, upkeep is n L (c_poly + c_deg + c_recycle) k_deg / N_A: a degraded copy's amino acids are reused, so replacing it costs re-polymerising (4) plus degrading (1) plus recycling (0.5) ATP per residue, not the 28 of making it from glucose.

What to review or decide

  1. Any protein can be actively removed in minutes. In real bacteria only proteins with a degradation tag (or a specific adaptor) are; most leave growing cells by dilution. This is a deliberate gameplay simplification; is it acceptable, or should removal be limited to proteins that real cells regulate this way?
  2. Dilution by growth is not modelled (the pool does not grow with the cell), nor the rise of degradation to ~5%/h in starvation (Willetts 1967).
  3. The 5-minute removal half-life is the fast end of measured tagged substrates (1–8 min at 37 °C, 38 min for tagged LacI at 25 °C). Low confidence.
  4. Unfolding cost not counted: ClpXP spends 10–500 extra ATP per substrate to unfold it. With L = 388 that is 0.03–1.3 ATP/aa on top of the 1 charged; the 1 ATP/aa could be low by up to 2×.
  5. The default instance is NhaA. The pool is generic, but its default parameters (n₀, L, footprint) are NhaA's; every other protein is an instance of it in the fermenter.
  6. min/max switches (at the goal, at ATP_ref) are kinks in the rates. The solver handles them (stability test passes), but they are non-smooth.
  7. Values to source: K_m_ATP 0.1 mM, footprint 15 nm², the membrane ceiling's 15% (all on the review list).

To learn more

  • Why m is relative: the game's sliders are multipliers of the calibrated cell, so a pool works in m and converts to copies with n₀.
  • Why the rise follows the mRNA: a protein's synthesis rate follows its mRNA level, which turns over in minutes (bacterial mRNA half-lives 1–10 min; Bernstein 2002), so a change of target reaches synthesis on that timescale.
  • Why upkeep is cheap compared with building: turnover recycles amino acids, so replacing a copy costs re-polymerising (≈4–5.5 ATP/aa) rather than making amino acids from glucose (≈24 more). Lynch & Marinov 2015 discuss these costs for whole cells.
  • Regulated proteolysis: ClpXP, Lon and FtsH (membrane) recognise tags such as the ssrA tag; see Kenniston 2003 and Purcell 2012 for energetics and temperature dependence.
